IS AUSTRALIA WATER-RICH?

Australia's Water Security and Consumption

Australia's water security is increasingly shaped by the balance between national water availability, growing demand through rising population, and long-term sustainability pressure. This visualisation explores how various factors such as rainfall availability, water storage, and sector consumption influences the nation's ability to maintian reliable and accessible water across states and industries. Throughout interactive visualisations here, it highlights changing water supply patterns, industry dependence on water resources, regional restriction pressure, and Australia's progress towards greater water efficiency through various movements and activities. Together, these insights provide a comprehensive overview of the challenges and resillience of Australia's future water supply and security.

HOW MUCH WATER DO AUSTRALIA RECEIVE?

National Rainfall Over the Years (1900-2025)

>

The stacked bar chart displays Australia's average annual rainfall data from 1900 to 2025 that is broken down by monthly contributions. A secondary timeline below displays the annual rainfall data across the 125-year period in one view. The total annual rainfall typically fluctuate between 300mm and 600mm with extreme outliers present as well. The wettest periods occured during the 1970s, peaking near 760mm in 1974 followed by 2000 and 2011 where annual average reached 700mm. The color-embedded legend showed consistent seasonal pattern where cooler months (darker colors) are the driest all year around while hotter months (lighter colors) have higher rainfalls. The all-year-around data highlights how Australia's rainfall consistently stayed volatile across the years, and have recently spiked again since 2020.

Data source: (BOM, 2026)
https://www.bom.gov.au/cgi-bin/climate/change/timeseries.cgi?graph=rain&area=aus&season=allmonths&ave_yr=T

Water Supply Proportion Over the Years (2015-2024)

The visualisation tracks Australia's water supply origins from 2015 to 2024, revealing a massive strucutral reliance on sea water reaching over 1.5 million GL annually. Surface water from dams and rivers serves as the primary buffer, expanding significantly to a peak in 2022 for all water types (high rainfall year) before declining in 2024 as drier climate patterns surged. On the other hand, groundwater maintains a very small proportion from the different water sources compared, highlighting its use as a 'last resort' rather than primary source. Total supply peaked in 2022, exceeding over 2 million GL in total. Over the years, the proportion of each water type remain relatively constant, with groundwater having a more stable and flat trend. Sea water experienced a relatively more drastic change compared to the other 2 types, suggesting that it acts as a more accessible source of water.

Data source: (ABS, 2024)
https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/environment/environmental-accounts/water-account-australia/latest-release

Water Use and Water Consumption by Sector (2024)

Water use is the amount of water taken for human and industrial activities that can be returned to the water source, while water consumption refers to the water that is permanently lost. Hydroelectricity has the largest gap with high water use but with a very low water consumption, emphasising its high usage which remain non-consumptive. Household has the highest volume of water use with low consumption while sectors including agriculture, other industries, and environment have equal water use and consumption ratio, highlighting how every water withdrawn by these sectors are entirely lost to the ecosystem.

Data source: (ABS, 2024)
https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/environment/environmental-accounts/water-account-australia/latest-release

Reuse-Water Used by Industries Over the Years (2021-2024)

Data source: (ABS, 2024)
https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/environment/environmental-accounts/water-account-australia/latest-release

The small multiples waterfall chart per sector showcases the amount of reuse water used by industries over the years. In 2021 to 2022, Australia was experiencing high rainfall leading to low dependence, hence low usage on recycled or reuse water for every sector (on average). This was folowed by its lowest point at 2023 which then rapidly surge in 2024 when dry conditions continue to persist resulting in heavy reliance on recycled water for every sector. These drastic changes were experienced mostly by the agriculture sector experiencing drastic changes throughout the years, and the mining sector where reuse water declined by over 78% in 2021 and 2022 due to high Australia's wettest periods. Contrasting this, utility service showed a continous increase throughout the years, building long-term supply. Manufacturing and households remained high with households experiencing slightly more drastic changes throughout the years compared to other sectors.

RISK AND FUTURE PRESSURE

Household Annual Water Consumption vs Water Restriction Levels (2024)

The map visualises the spatial relationship between regional household water consumption acrosss Australian states and localized water restriction levels. Severity of restriction level starts from level 1 to level 3, with PWCM representing light daily water measures and no restrictions having the best condition. The choropleth shading highlights how households in the Northern Territory and Western Australia consumes the highest volume of water annually per capita, while more densely populated eastern states have significantly lower averages. Here, the colored dots highlight how despite having a lower statewide household water consumption baseline, the eastern states, particularly NSW and QLD face a much more severe supply pressure, with an overall level 2 and 3 restrictions. States in the west and north manage their high consumption rates under permanent water conservation measures (PWCM) or no restrictions at all on average which may be due to different groundwater dependencies and climates.

WATER STORAGE AND RESILLIENCE

Population Growth Against Accessible Water Storage (2024)

The slope chart visualises the safety buffer or supply strain each major cities in Australia faces. On average, a person consumes 110 KL water (0.1 ML) in a year. The population demand is derived using this parameter in determining how much water is needed for residential consumption. Having the highest population and demand, Sydney has the largest water storage while Hobart has the lowest water capacity storage. Meanwhile, Perth has the highest water storage availability to supply its people, relative to its lower demand. Contrasting this, Hobart has a relatively higher demand than water storage, showing high vulnerability of meeting water needs for the population. There is also a lack of storage in Adelaide from its almost completely flat line. When compared throughout the years, the demand has significantly increased, creating a flatter slope of rapidly growing cities like Sydney and Melbourne.

Data source: (BOM, 2024)
https://www.bom.gov.au/water/dashboards/#/water-storages/summary/state

Water Storage vs Rainfall (2024)

The visualisation illustrates a clear geographic divide in water vulnerability, revealing how different regions balance their natural water supply with storage. In the northern and eastern areas such as NT and QLD, high average annual rainfall naturally keeps local water storage highly saturated and stable including capital cities in these areas, making these regions more resilient. Conversely, low rainfall regions like SA and WA has a strong gap between urban water infrastructure and state resources. The gap between city and state storage in SA and WS shows how urban water storage for city faces severe deficit due to lack of natural rainfall. In unusual climates, localised water storage are vulnerable, forcing urban cities to rely heavily on importing water from wider state infrastructure to cope with low annual rainfall.

AUSTRALIA'S MOVEMENT TOWARDS WATER EFFICIENCY

Australia's Water Efficiency Movement Target and Progress (2026)

The bullet chart tracks Australia's 2026 water recovery and efficiency progress against statutory targets like under the Murray-Darling Basin Plan framework. Australia has nearly achieved its "Bridging the Gap" environmental water recovery, with over 99.4% of progress completed for surface water and 95.1% for groundwater. In contrast, the "450 GL" efficiency target which aims to recover water through modernization projects to reduce water usage is lagging drastically behind at just 54.7% of progress. These efficiency targets are crucial during dry seasons, particularly for agricultural systems and regional communities who face harsher socioeconomic strain due to infrastructure inefficiency and unavailability.
